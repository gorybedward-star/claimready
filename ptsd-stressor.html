Your PTSD stressor: how VA accepts that it happened

To service connect PTSD, VA must accept that the stressful event you describe really happened during service. VA calls this conceding the stressor. The rules depend on what kind of event it was. For some kinds, your own statement can be enough. For others, VA needs something more. This article explains each kind in plain English, so you know what VA looks for and what you can give it.

Based on public VA information: the regulation 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f) and VA's procedures manual, M21-1, Part VIII, Subpart iv, Chapter 1, Section A, Topic 3 (topic changed Jun 30, 2025; article updated Mar 17, 2026). Start here

Three things VA needs for PTSD

  1. A diagnosis of PTSD that meets the standard VA uses (DSM-5).
  2. A medical link between your current symptoms and an event in service.
  3. Credible supporting evidence that the event happened. This article is about this third part.
  • VA decides whether the event happened. The examiner decides whether the event is enough to support a PTSD diagnosis and whether your symptoms are related to it.
  • You do not need a diagnosis first. VA's guidance says a PTSD diagnosis is not required before VA starts checking a stressor.
  • There is no special form required to claim a stressor. VA can learn about it from VA Form 21-0781, a written statement, your military records, your medical records, or what you tell an examiner.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f); § 4.125(a) VA guidance M21-1 VIII.iv.1.A.3.a, d

The key question

When your own statement can be enough

For some kinds of stressors, the law lets your own account establish that the event happened, without other proof. Two conditions apply every time:

  • The event must be consistent with your service: the circumstances, conditions, or hardships of that service, or for a fear-based or drone stressor, the places, types, and circumstances of your service.
  • There must be no clear and convincing evidence to the contrary.

Combat

Combat stressors

Engaging in combat with the enemy means personally taking part in an actual fight or encounter with a military foe, hostile unit, or enemy weapon. It includes being present as a fighter or as someone doing duty in support of fighters, such as giving medical care to the wounded.

What helps VA accept it

  • A combat decoration (list below). VA presumes combat unless clear and convincing evidence shows otherwise.
  • Other credible evidence of combat. A decoration is not the only acceptable proof.
  • If you have a combat decoration but did not describe the event, VA's guidance says to assume the stressor is combat related and request your personnel records.

Other records that can show combat

  • Unit and organizational histories
  • Hostile fire pay or hazard pay records
  • Records of your military job (occupation)
  • Statements from people who served with you
Decorations VA treats as evidence of combat
  • Air Force Achievement Medal with "V"
  • Air Force Combat Action Medal
  • Air Force Commendation Medal with "V"
  • Air Force Cross
  • Air Medal with "V"
  • Army Commendation Medal with "V"
  • Bronze Star Medal with "V"
  • "C" device on other meritorious awards
  • Combat Action Badge
  • Combat Action Ribbon
  • Navy Achievement Medal with "V" (before Feb 1969, in place of the Combat Action Ribbon)
  • Combat Aircrew Insignia
  • Combat Infantryman Badge
  • Combat Medical Badge
  • Distinguished Flying Cross
  • Distinguished Service Cross
  • Fleet Marine Force Combat Operations Insignia
  • Joint Service Commendation Medal with "V"
  • Medal of Honor
  • Navy Commendation Medal with "V"
  • Navy Cross
  • Parachutist Badge with Combat Jump Device
  • Purple Heart
  • Silver Star

Fear of hostile activity

Fear of hostile military or terrorist activity

This covers service where an attack was always possible, even if you were never in a firefight. You experienced, witnessed, or were confronted with an event or situation involving actual or threatened death or serious injury, or a threat to your own or someone else's physical safety, and you reacted with fear, helplessness, or horror.

  • Improvised explosive device (IED)
  • Vehicle-borne explosive
  • Incoming artillery, rocket, or mortar fire
  • Grenade
  • Small arms fire, including suspected sniper fire
  • Attack on friendly military aircraft

What helps VA accept it

  • Records (such as your DD Form 214) showing you served in an area of potential hostile activity.
  • A VA or VA-contracted psychiatrist or psychologist confirming the event is adequate for a PTSD diagnosis and your symptoms are related to it.
  • VA's guidance says to request your personnel records before or with the exam.

Medals that point to the place

  • Generally show it: Vietnam Service or Campaign Medal, Kuwait Liberation Medal, Iraq Campaign Medal, Afghanistan Campaign Medal.
  • Generally do not: National Defense Service Medal, Armed Forces Service Medal, Global War on Terrorism Service Medal. They do not name an area.
  • Not by itself: the Global War on Terrorism Expeditionary Medal. VA weighs it with other evidence.
Examples of places VA's guidance names
  • Along the Korean demilitarized zone (DMZ), hostile since the 1953 armistice. Service on U.S. bases elsewhere in South Korea generally has not been.
  • Aboard ship in the offshore waters of Vietnam, or in Thailand, when you received the Vietnam Service Medal or Vietnam Campaign Medal.
  • The list is not complete. VA looks at each case.

What does not meet the rule: worrying about a future deployment, or learning that someone died far away in a hostile area.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(3) VA guidance M21-1 VIII.iv.1.A.3.f, i, k

Drone crews

Armed drone aircraft crew duty

VA's guidance treats duty as a crew member of an armed drone aircraft, such as the Predator or Reaper, the same way as a fear-based stressor.

  • What helps: your DD Form 214 or other service records showing that duty.
  • A VA or VA-contracted psychiatrist or psychologist must confirm the stressor is adequate and your symptoms are related to it.
  • VA's guidance says to request your personnel records before or with the exam.

VA guidance M21-1 VIII.iv.1.A.3.b, l

POWs and in-service diagnosis

Former POWs, and PTSD diagnosed during service

  • Former prisoners of war: your own account can establish an event related to your captivity if it fits the circumstances of that service. "Prisoner of war" has a legal definition in 38 C.F.R. § 3.1(y). ClaimReady's Former POWs article covers the presumptions that also apply.
  • PTSD diagnosed in service: if a provider diagnosed PTSD while you were serving, your account of a related in-service event can be enough. Your in-service mental health records matter here; they may not be stored with your other medical records, so tell VA where you were treated.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(1), (4); § 3.1(y) VA guidance M21-1 VIII.iv.1.A.3.b

Other events

Non-combat events

These are stressful events that were not combat and not fear of enemy activity. VA's guidance says they always need credible supporting evidence; your statement alone is not enough.

  • Natural disasters
  • A life-threatening illness of you or someone close to you
  • Burn ward duty
  • Graves registration duty
  • Seeing someone harmed, not by the enemy
  • Ship sinkings, explosions, or plane crashes in routine drills
  • Friendly fire on a gunnery range in training

Personal trauma

Assault and military sexual trauma (MST)

Claims based on personal assault, including MST, follow separate rules. They are built around the fact that many assaults are never reported. Evidence from outside military records, and changes in behavior after the event, can support the claim. VA must tell you about these other kinds of evidence before it denies the claim.

  • ClaimReady's PTSD and MST article explains these rules, including the behavior changes VA calls "markers."
  • VA provides free care for conditions related to MST, whether or not you file a claim or have records of the event.
  • If talking about this is hard right now, you can reach the Veterans Crisis Line any time: dial 988 and press 1.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(5) VA guidance M21-1 VIII.iv.1.B

When more than your statement is needed

What "credible supporting evidence" means

When your own statement cannot establish the stressor by itself, VA looks for evidence that, taken together:

  1. documents that you took part in the event,
  2. shows you served in the immediate area at the time it happened, and
  3. supports your description of what happened.

Good news in VA's guidance

  • Not every detail has to be proven.
  • Evidence can be enough if it implies you were personally exposed, such as records showing your unit came under attack.
  • VA looks at the evidence as a whole.
  • The evidence does not have to come from service records.

Kinds of evidence that can help

  • Statements from people who served with you (buddy statements)
  • Letters or emails home, journals, and photos from the time
  • Unit histories, ship logs, and after-action or accident reports
  • News reports of the event
  • Police, hospital, or civilian medical records
  • Your service personnel and medical records

VA guidance M21-1 VIII.iv.1.A.1.f; 1.A.3.c, m

What you can do

Give VA enough to look it up

When VA has to search military records for your event, its guidance says it needs at least four details. Use this as a checklist when you write your statement. Your checks are not saved.

Minimum details for a records search
  • If someone was killed or wounded, their name and unit help, but VA's guidance says they are not required if you describe the event.
  • For well-known events, such as the Tet Offensive, VA can fill in the date and place itself.
  • If VA writes to ask for more details, answer it. VA's guidance allows 30 days for a reply before the claim moves on.
  • VA Form 21-0781 is the form VA uses to collect these details, but a clear written statement can also work.
Events that records research cannot confirm

VA's guidance says some events are not sent for military records research because records would not show them, such as events that almost happened and mistreatment of enemy prisoners. That does not mean the event did not matter. The rules above about your own statement, and other kinds of evidence, can still apply.

VA guidance M21-1 VIII.iv.1.A.3.d, o, p

Behind the decision

How VA reviews a stressor, in plain steps

  1. VA works out what kind of event it is.It reads everything: your statement or 21-0781, service records, medical records, and other evidence.
  2. VA asks whether your statement alone is enough.For combat, POW, in-service diagnosis, fear-based, and drone stressors, it can be. If so, VA moves to the exam step.
  3. If not, VA gathers the evidence you pointed to and searches what is available.This is where buddy statements and records you name matter most.
  4. If it still is not confirmed, VA checks whether you gave the four details.If yes, VA can ask for a deeper search of military unit records. If not, VA asks you for them.
  5. VA's rating staff makes the final call on whether the stressor is conceded.
  6. VA decides whether an exam is needed.See C&P exams: the complete guide.

VA guidance M21-1 VIII.iv.1.A.3.c–d

Example

How this might look for Dana

Fictional example

Dana served in Iraq in 2006 and received the Iraq Campaign Medal. Dana describes repeated mortar attacks on the base. Dana also describes seeing a fellow soldier badly hurt in a vehicle rollover during training back in the United States.

  • The mortar attacks are a fear-based stressor. Dana's service records place Dana in Iraq. If a VA or VA-contracted psychologist confirms the event is adequate for a PTSD diagnosis and Dana's symptoms are related to it, Dana's own statement can be enough.
  • The training rollover is a non-combat event. Dana's statement alone is not enough. A buddy statement from someone in the unit and the accident report could provide credible supporting evidence. Dana gives the base, the approximate months, and the unit.

This is a made-up example to show how the rules fit together. It is not a real VA decision and does not predict any outcome.

Common misunderstandings

What people often get wrong

"I need a combat medal."
No. A decoration is strong evidence of combat, but VA's guidance says it is not the only acceptable proof.
"Every detail has to be proven."
No. Corroboration of every detail is not required, and evidence can be enough if it implies you were personally exposed.
"If my service records don't mention it, it's over."
No. Supporting evidence can come from sources other than service records.
"I need a diagnosis before VA checks my stressor."
No. VA's guidance says a diagnosis is not a prerequisite to start checking.
"Being deployed proves my PTSD."
Not by itself. Service in a hostile area can support a fear-based stressor, but VA still needs a diagnosis, a VA or contract psychiatrist's or psychologist's confirmation, and a link to your symptoms.
