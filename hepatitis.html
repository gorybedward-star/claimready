Hepatitis claims: blood tests, risk factors, and ratings

Hepatitis claims work differently from most claims. VA needs a blood test that names the type of hepatitis, and it looks at every way you could have been infected, before, during, and after service. This article explains the three types VA rates, the tests that prove a diagnosis, the risk factors VA weighs, how drug use is handled, what a good medical opinion covers, and how hepatitis is rated.

Based on public sources: 38 U.S.C. §§ 105 and 1110, 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.102, 3.159, 3.301, 3.303, 3.957, 4.112, and 4.114, published court decisions, and VA's procedures manual, M21-1, Part V, Subpart iii, Chapter 6, Section 3. The basics

Three types of viral hepatitis

Hepatitis means inflammation of the liver. VA's manual describes three main viral types for rating purposes. Older records may use the older names shown below.

TypeHow it spreadsWhat usually happens
Hepatitis A (HAV)
once called "infectious hepatitis"		Fecal-oral route, such as contaminated food or waterA short (acute) illness that seldom leaves lasting damage. Service connection needs evidence of chronic residuals.
Hepatitis B (HBV)
once called "serum hepatitis"		Blood and blood products; sexual contactUsually clears after the acute phase (90 to 95% of cases in VA's manual), though the acute illness can be severe. Becomes chronic in 5 to 10%. Cirrhosis and liver cancer can develop. A vaccine exists.
Hepatitis C (HCV)
once called "non-A, non-B hepatitis"		Infected bloodOften no symptoms at first. Becomes chronic in about 80%. It is usually found by chance, many years later.

History note from VA's manual: infectious hepatitis was common in World War II, especially after the 1942 yellow fever vaccine and in the Mediterranean Theater.

VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.6.3.a

Proving the diagnosis

VA needs blood tests that name the type

A diagnosis of "hepatitis" alone is not enough. VA's manual calls for serology (blood tests for the virus or the body's antibodies to it) to confirm the type, and liver function tests to show how severe it is. The decision should name the type that is service connected.

Hepatitis C

  • An antibody test (anti-HCV, by EIA or ELISA) is usually first. A positive antibody alone does not prove chronic hepatitis C.
  • A positive HCV RNA test (the virus itself) is enough by itself to confirm hepatitis C.
  • Older records may show a RIBA test used to confirm a positive antibody.

Hepatitis B

  • HBsAg (surface antigen) shows active infection. If it lasts more than 6 months, that suggests chronic infection or carrier status.
  • An old "Australia antigen" result is the same marker and is enough to confirm hepatitis B.
  • Antibody tests (anti-HBc, anti-HBs) help show past infection or immunity.

Hepatitis A

  • Anti-HAV antibodies appear about a month after the illness and last for life.
  • A positive result shows a past infection, not a current disease.

Liver biopsy, ultrasound, and CT scans can show liver damage, but they cannot tell which virus caused it.

Medical background, not from VA

Since 2013, the CDC has recommended confirming a positive hepatitis C antibody with an HCV RNA test. The RIBA test VA's manual mentions is no longer available in the U.S. A positive antibody with a negative RNA test usually means a past infection that cleared, or a false positive.

The heart of the claim

Risk factors: how you might have been infected

Hepatitis B and C share most risk factors. VA's manual lists the ones it treats as medically recognized, along with the kinds of records that can show them. The question is usually not whether you have hepatitis, but which exposure most likely caused it.

Risk factorWhy it mattersRecords that can help
Blood transfusion before 1992 (hepatitis C) or before 1975 (hepatitis B); organ transplant before 1992; kidney dialysisBlood screening for hepatitis C did not exist until the virus was identified in 1989 and became much more effective in 1992.Hospital records from service, especially surgical reports, anesthesia sheets, and post-operative notes
Tattoos, body piercing, or acupuncture with non-sterile needlesUnsterilized equipment can carry the virus.Entry and separation exams that do or do not list tattoos or piercings, which can help show when they happened
Accidental blood exposure, such as a needlestick or blood in the eyes or mouthCommon for health care workers, combat medics, and corpsmenPersonnel records showing your job, duty assignments, and any exposure reports
Sharing razors or toothbrushesDirect contact with bloodRarely written down. Statements from people who served with you can help, weighed with the rest of the evidence.
High-risk sexual activityRisk is relatively low but rises with multiple partners.Health assessments or treatment records for sexually transmitted infections
Injection drug use or intranasal cocaine useShared needles or straws can carry blood.Drug treatment records. See how VA handles drug use.
Vaccination with a jet air gun injectorVA's manual notes one documented case of hepatitis B spread this way and says spread of hepatitis C is biologically possible, though not scientifically documented.A medical opinion that links hepatitis to an air gun must discuss all possible sources and explain why the air gun is the likely one.

If the evidence for a risk factor in service is about as strong as the evidence for one before or after service, the tie goes to you.

This is the benefit-of-the-doubt rule. In Andrews v. McDonough (2021), the Veterans Court reversed a hepatitis C denial outright. The veteran described air gun vaccinations and dental work in service, the Board did not dispute them, VA had found no risk factors before or after service, and the evidence was at least evenly balanced.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.102 Court Andrews v. McDonough, 34 Vet. App. 151 (2021) VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.6.3.e

What to expect

VA will ask you about risk factors

The standard claim form does not ask about hepatitis risk factors, so VA's manual says to ask in every hepatitis claim, even when hepatitis was diagnosed in service, unless you have already given a complete history. You will likely get a letter or questionnaire asking about risk factors before, during, and after service.

  • Answer every question, including the ones about life before and after service. VA is required to look at the whole picture, and an unanswered question can slow the claim or lead to a weaker opinion.
  • Be honest and specific. Give dates and places where you can. A clear, consistent account is more believable than a vague one.
  • Point to records. Tell VA about in-service surgeries, hospital stays, injuries with bleeding, or medical jobs.
  • Your own statement is evidence. In Andrews, the court weighed the veteran's statement that he had no risk factors outside service.

VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.6.3.f Court Andrews v. McDonough, 34 Vet. App. 151 (2021)

A sensitive topic

How VA handles drug use in a hepatitis claim

Many veterans have drug use somewhere in their history. It matters in a hepatitis claim because of one rule in the law, but it does not end the claim on its own.

The rule in the law

For claims filed after October 31, 1990, VA cannot pay compensation for a disability that results from a veteran's own abuse of drugs. The regulation defines drug abuse to include using illegal drugs, misusing prescription drugs, and using substances to experience their intoxicating effects.

The steps in VA's manual when injection drug or intranasal cocaine use is confirmed in service

  1. VA does not assume drug use caused it. It must look for every other risk factor too.
  2. If drug use is the only confirmed risk factor in service, VA denies service connection.This is the rule above at work.
  3. If there are other confirmed risk factors in service, VA asks a medical examiner which one is at least as likely as not the cause.
  4. VA reads the opinion:
    • Drug use is the cause: denied.
    • Another in-service risk factor carries equal or greater weight: granted, with the doubt resolved in your favor.
    • The examiner cannot say which is more likely: VA weighs all the evidence and applies the benefit of the doubt if it is evenly balanced.

When drug use was caused by a service-connected condition

The Federal Circuit held in Allen v. Principi (2001) that the drug abuse rule does not bar compensation when the substance use itself results from a service-connected condition, such as PTSD or chronic pain. In that situation, hepatitis from the drug use may be service connected as a secondary condition. The court required clear medical evidence that the service-connected condition caused the substance use.

Line of duty and substance use · Secondary service connection

What about the "infections coinciding with injection" language?

An older part of the regulation says infections that coincide with drug injection are not considered "willful misconduct." That rule is separate. For claims filed after October 31, 1990, the drug abuse bar in the law and in 38 C.F.R. § 3.301(d) still applies, which is why VA's manual denies a claim when drug use is the only in-service risk factor. If this applies to you, talk with an accredited representative.

Law 38 U.S.C. §§ 105(a), 1110; 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.102, 3.301(c)(3), (d) Court Allen v. Principi, 237 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2001) VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.6.3.g

Already service connected

Older awards where drug use was the only risk factor

Some veterans were service connected for hepatitis when drug use was the only apparent in-service risk factor. VA's manual has special steps when one of them asks for a higher rating.

Awarded on or before Oct 31, 1990

The award was proper under the rules at the time, so service connection continues. But under a VA General Counsel opinion, the rating cannot be increased when the award was based on drug abuse.

Awarded after Oct 31, 1990

VA may get a medical opinion on whether drug use caused the hepatitis. If the opinion cannot decide, or points to another in-service risk factor, service connection continues and an increase can be granted if the evidence supports it. If the evidence clearly shows drug use was the cause, VA checks whether the award is protected; if it is not, VA may propose to sever it.

Protections that apply: service connection in effect for 10 years or more generally cannot be severed except for fraud or a lack of required service. Before that, VA can sever only if the award was "clearly and unmistakably erroneous," VA carries that burden, and you get advance notice and 60 days to send evidence. If you receive a proposal, contact an accredited representative right away.

Law Pub. L. 101-508, § 8052; 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.105(d), 3.957; VAOPGCPREC 2-1998 VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.6.3.h

The exam

The exam and the medical opinion

For hepatitis B or C, the examiner usually completes the Hepatitis, Cirrhosis and Other Liver Conditions questionnaire (DBQ) and gives an opinion on which risk factor most likely caused the infection.

What the examiner should consider

  • All relevant evidence in your file
  • The risk factors you reported
  • Every risk factor the records confirm, whether you named it or not, before, during, and after service
  • For a link to service, only risk factors confirmed by the evidence

Signs an opinion may be inadequate

  • No confirming blood test for the diagnosis
  • Incomplete findings or symptoms
  • Does not address every known risk factor
  • Links hepatitis to a risk factor the record does not confirm
  • Links it to something not medically recognized as a way to catch hepatitis

If you believe the opinion missed something, you can point it out to VA, send your own doctor's opinion, or ask for a review after a decision. See Medical opinions and Supplemental Claims.

VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.6.3.j–k

Ratings

How hepatitis B and C are rated

Hepatitis C is listed under diagnostic code 7354 but rated using the criteria for chronic liver disease without cirrhosis (code 7345). Hepatitis B is rated directly under 7345. These criteria took effect May 19, 2024; older decisions used earlier criteria.

  • 100%
    Progressive chronic liver disease that requires both parenteral (injected or IV) antiviral therapy and parenteral immune-modulating therapy such as interferon. Continues for 6 months after treatment ends, then VA re-examines.
  • 60%
    Progressive chronic liver disease that requires continuous medication and causes substantial weight loss, plus at least two of: daily fatigue, malaise, loss of appetite, enlarged liver, itching, joint pain.
  • 40%
    The same, with minor weight loss.
  • 20%
    Chronic liver disease with at least one of: intermittent fatigue, malaise, loss of appetite, enlarged liver, or itching.
  • 0%
    A past history of liver disease, with no current symptoms.
What counts as substantial or minor weight loss
  • Substantial: involuntary loss of more than 20% of your baseline weight for 3 months, with reduced ability to care for yourself or work.
  • Minor: involuntary loss of 10% to 20% for 3 months, with digestive symptoms and reduced self-care, work, or food intake.
  • Baseline: your documented average weight for the 2 years before the illness began; otherwise your last discharge exam weight; otherwise an ideal body weight using the method most favorable to you.
  • Complications are rated separately. Cirrhosis, liver cancer, and other effects are rated under their own codes, but the same symptoms cannot be counted twice.
  • If doctors recommend the 100% treatments but you cannot have them for medical reasons, VA rates under the cirrhosis code instead.
  • Hepatitis B and other liver conditions under code 7345 cannot be combined with many other digestive ratings; VA rates the main condition and may raise it.
Medical background, not from VA

Most hepatitis C treatment today is taken by mouth, not injected. Whether a particular treatment meets the 100% wording is a question for VA. Ask your doctor what your treatment is and how it is given.

Law 38 C.F.R. §§ 4.14, 4.112, 4.114 (DCs 7312, 7345, 7354), as amended by 89 FR 19735 (Mar 20, 2024; effective May 19, 2024)

Ratings

When 0% applies to hepatitis C, and when it does not

VA's manual says 0% is only for hepatitis C that is without symptoms and has a past history of liver disease.

If the medical evidence showsIs 0% right?
Even mild symptoms from hepatitis CNo. You have symptoms.
Liver damage on liver function tests, biopsy, or other testingNo. The infection is not healed.
Treatment worked, RNA tests are negative, no symptoms, and no liver damageYes. VA's manual adds that the virus may stay dormant and flare later.

What this means for you: tell the examiner about every symptom, even mild or on-and-off ones like fatigue or itching, and make sure recent lab work is in the record.

Medical background, not from VA

Current medical guidance generally treats a lasting negative RNA result after modern treatment (a "sustained virologic response") as a cure, though reinfection is possible. VA's manual takes a more cautious view. Either way, the rating follows your symptoms and test results.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 4.114, DC 7345, 7354 VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.6.3.l

Example

How this might look

Fictional example

A Navy veteran served as a corpsman from 1984 to 1988. In 2023 routine screening found a positive hepatitis C antibody, and a follow-up HCV RNA test was positive. The veteran also got a tattoo two years after leaving service.

  • Diagnosis: the positive RNA test confirms hepatitis C.
  • In service: personnel records show corpsman duty, and the veteran describes a needlestick while treating a bleeding patient. A shipmate's statement backs that up.
  • After service: the tattoo is a post-service risk factor, so VA must weigh it too.
  • The opinion: the examiner reviews both and explains which is at least as likely as not the cause. If the evidence for each is evenly balanced, the benefit of the doubt goes to the veteran.
  • The rating: treatment cleared the virus, but the veteran still reports intermittent fatigue. VA weighs that symptom and its connection to the liver disease.

This is a made-up example to show how the rules fit together. It is not a real VA decision and does not predict any outcome.

Common misunderstandings

What people often get wrong

"Any drug use means an automatic denial."
No. VA must look at every other risk factor, and drug use caused by a service-connected condition can be a path to secondary service connection.
"I had hepatitis in service, so my hepatitis C is automatic."
Not if the earlier finding was hepatitis A or B. Each type is caught separately. An old "non-A, non-B" diagnosis is much stronger evidence.
"A positive antibody test proves I have hepatitis C."
Not by itself. A positive RNA test is what confirms it.
"I should leave out risk factors after service."
VA is required to look for them, and leaving them out can hurt your credibility. The benefit of the doubt applies when the evidence is balanced.
"Air gun vaccinations will get my claim granted."
They can be considered, but an opinion must discuss every possible source and explain why the air gun is the likely one.
"My hepatitis C was treated, so I'm stuck at 0%."
Only if you have no symptoms and no liver damage. Lingering symptoms or damage can support a higher rating.
