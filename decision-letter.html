Decode your decision letter

VA rating decisions use the same phrases again and again: "no nexus," "not shown in service," "evaluation continued." Look up a phrase from your letter to see what it means in plain English, the law behind it, and which next step may fit. This guide explains common wording; it does not read your letter or decide your claim. You don't upload anything, and nothing you type here is saved or sent.

What every decision notice must tell you

Federal law lists what VA's written notice of a decision must include. If one is missing, that is worth asking about.

  1. The issues decided
  2. A summary of the evidence considered
  3. A summary of the laws and regulations that apply
  4. Any findings in your favor
  5. For a denial, the element or elements not met
  6. If applicable, what it takes to grant service connection or the next higher rating
  7. How to get the evidence used in the decision
  8. Your review options
How to read a denial

Most service connection denials turn on one of three questions: Do you have a current condition? Did something happen in service? Is there a link between them? The favorable findings tell you which ones are already answered yes. The reasons tell you which one is not.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5104(b); 38 C.F.R. § 3.103(f)(1)–(8) Court decision Shedden v. Principi, 381 F.3d 1163 (Fed. Cir. 2004)

Parts of the letter

The sections every rating decision has, and what to look for in each.

DecisionThe list of what VA decided

What it means

Each issue is numbered with its outcome: granted, denied, continued, or deferred. A grant shows the percentage and the effective date. VA usually names the condition with the examiner's diagnosis and puts your words in parentheses, for example "tinnitus (claimed as ringing in the ears)."

A next step that may fit

  • Check that every condition you claimed appears, either decided or deferred. A claimed issue with no outcome is worth asking about.
  • If the name looks different from what you claimed, the words in parentheses tell you it is the same claim.
  • Read Does your decision cover everything? for the nine questions to ask.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5104(b)(1); 38 C.F.R. § 3.103(f)(1) VA guidance M21-1, Part V, Subpart iv, Chapter 1 (rating decision format)

EvidenceWhat VA looked at

You may also see: Evidence considered

What it means

A list of everything VA reviewed, usually with dates: service records, VA and private treatment records, exam reports, statements, and forms. VA must give a summary of the evidence it considered.

A next step that may fit

  • Make sure everything you sent is on the list. If a key record is missing, that matters for the next step you choose.
  • If VA had the record but misread it, a Higher-Level Review guide (20-0996) fits. If the record was never in the file, a Supplemental Claim guide (20-0995) lets you add it.
  • You can ask VA for a copy of the evidence used. The notice must explain how to get it.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5104(b)(2), (6); 38 C.F.R. § 3.103(f)(2), (7) VA guidance M21-1, Part V, Subpart iv, Chapter 1 (rating decision format)

Requested, but not receivedRecords VA tried and failed to get

You may also see: We asked for … but did not receive

What it means

VA asked someone, such as a private doctor or a records office, for records and did not get them. When VA cannot get records, it must tell you which ones, what it tried, what it will do next, and that you are ultimately responsible for getting them to VA.

A next step that may fit

  • If you can get those records yourself, sending them with a Supplemental Claim guide (20-0995) puts them in the record.
  • If you told VA about records and the letter does not show that VA tried to get them, that may be a duty-to-assist question. A Higher-Level Review guide (20-0996) can look at it.
  • The Form 21-4142 guide explains how to authorize VA to request private records.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5103A(b); 38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c), (e)(1)

Reasons for decisionWhy VA decided each issue the way it did

What it means

This is the most important part of the letter. For each issue, VA explains the law it applied and how the evidence fits. For a grant, it explains the rating, what the next higher rating requires, and why it picked the effective date. For a denial, it names the requirement that was not met.

A next step that may fit

  • For each denial, underline the sentence that names the missing element. The entries under Why a claim was denied explain the common ones.
  • For each grant, find the sentence about the next higher rating. That is the gap between your symptoms and the next level.
  • If a theory you raised (such as secondary) is not discussed, that is worth raising in a review.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5104(b)(3), (5), (7); 38 C.F.R. § 3.103(f)(3), (5), (6) VA guidance M21-1, Part V, Subpart iv, Chapter 1 (rating decision format)

Favorable findingsFacts VA already decided in your favor

You may also see: The evidence shows …; We have made the following favorable findings

What it means

A favorable finding is a fact or conclusion VA decided for you, even in a denial. For example: "Your service treatment records show you injured your left knee in June 1998," or "You have a current diagnosis of sleep apnea." VA must list them in the notice.

A next step that may fit

  • These are already proven. Later VA decision makers and the Board are bound by them, so new evidence can focus only on what is still missing.
  • Example: if the findings say you have a diagnosis and an injury in service, the denial was about the link. A medical opinion on the link is what would matter. See Medical opinions explained.
  • The statute lets a later decision maker set a finding aside only with clear and convincing evidence to the contrary. VA's regulation phrases the bar as evidence showing clear and unmistakable error in the finding.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5104(b)(4); 38 U.S.C. § 5104A; 38 C.F.R. § 3.103(f)(4); 38 C.F.R. § 3.104(c)

ReferencesThe rules VA applied

You may also see: Laws and regulations; Title 38 Code of Federal Regulations

What it means

A list or summary of the statutes and regulations VA used. "38 C.F.R." is the Code of Federal Regulations, Title 38, where VA's rules live. Part 3 covers service connection and effective dates; Part 4 is the rating schedule.

A next step that may fit

  • Look up any section on eCFR (links in this guide go there). The rule's exact words often show what evidence would meet it.
  • If the letter cites a rating code (for example 5260 for limited knee bending), the criteria for each percentage are in Part 4. See Lesson 5: How ratings work.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5104(b)(3); 38 C.F.R. § 3.103(f)(3)

Your review optionsHow to ask for another look

You may also see: What you should do if you disagree with our decision

What it means

The notice must summarize your review options: a Supplemental Claim, a Higher-Level Review, or a Board appeal. Usually you have one year from the date of the notice to choose one and keep your original effective date.

A next step that may fit

  • See Which review fits below, and Lesson 6: You got a decision.
  • Only one review option at a time can be pending for the same issue.
  • An accredited representative (VSO, agent, or attorney) can help you choose. VSOs help at no cost.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5104(a); 38 C.F.R. § 3.103(f)(8); 38 C.F.R. § 3.2500(b), (c)

Why a claim was denied

Each denial phrase points to one missing piece. Find the piece, and you know what evidence would matter.

No current diagnosisMissing piece: a current condition

You may also see: The evidence does not show a current disability; not shown by the evidence of record

What it means

Service connection needs a disability that exists now (or at some point while the claim was pending). VA found no diagnosis, or found symptoms alone without an underlying condition. Pain alone can count as a disability if it causes functional impairment, according to a 2018 federal court decision.

A next step that may fit

  • Evidence that would matter: a diagnosis from a treating provider, or treatment records showing the condition during the claim period.
  • If an exam was not done, or the examiner did not test the claimed condition, see Medical opinions explained and Supplemental Claims and new exams.
  • New evidence goes with a Supplemental Claim guide (20-0995).

Law 38 U.S.C. § 1110; 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(a) Court decision Brammer v. Derwinski, 3 Vet. App. 223 (1992); McClain v. Nicholson, 21 Vet. App. 319 (2007); Saunders v. Wilkie, 886 F.3d 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2018)

Not shown in serviceMissing piece: something in service

You may also see: There is no record of … during military service; The evidence does not show an event, disease, or injury in service

What it means

VA did not find an injury, disease, or event during service. Service treatment records are one source, but not the only one: VA must consider all lay and medical evidence, including your own statement and statements from people who served with you.

A next step that may fit

  • Evidence that would matter: buddy statements, letters home, personnel records, unit records, or your own detailed statement of what happened, when, and where.
  • For combat veterans, VA accepts satisfactory lay evidence of an injury in combat if it fits the circumstances of that service.
  • For conditions VA presumes, such as PACT Act conditions, the in-service piece may be met by where and when you served. See Presumptive service connection.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 1154(a), (b); 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(a); 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(d) VA guidance What evidence does VA need?

No nexusMissing piece: the link to service

You may also see: not related to your military service; The evidence does not show a link; less likely than not

What it means

VA accepted (or did not dispute) that you have a condition and that something happened in service, but found no link between them. Usually this follows a medical opinion that said the link is "less likely than not" (below a 50% probability).

A next step that may fit

  • Read the examiner's rationale, not just the conclusion. An opinion that ignores your records or your statements, or gives no reasons, carries less weight. See Medical opinions explained.
  • Evidence that would matter: a medical opinion from a provider who reviewed your history and explains why the link is at least as likely as not. That goes with a Supplemental Claim guide (20-0995).
  • If VA relied on an opinion with a clear gap that was already visible in the file, a Higher-Level Review guide (20-0996) can look at it.
  • Check the favorable findings first: they tell you the other pieces are already proven.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(a), (d); 38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)(4) Court decision Shedden v. Principi, 381 F.3d 1163 (Fed. Cir. 2004) (the three elements); Nieves-Rodriguez v. Peake, 22 Vet. App. 295 (2008) (weight of opinions)

not proximately due to, the result of, or aggravated byMissing piece: the link to a service-connected condition

You may also see: Service connection on a secondary basis is denied

What it means

You claimed the condition was caused by a condition that is already service connected. VA found neither cause nor aggravation (being made worse beyond its natural course). Both questions must be answered; an opinion that addresses only "caused by" is incomplete.

A next step that may fit

  • Check whether the opinion answered both caused-by and aggravated-by. If it skipped aggravation, that is a known gap a review can raise.
  • Evidence that would matter: a medical opinion explaining how the service-connected condition caused or worsened this one.
  • See Secondary service connection.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(a), (b) Court decision El-Amin v. Shinseki, 26 Vet. App. 136 (2013) (aggravation must be addressed)

existed prior to serviceBefore service: was it made worse?

You may also see: preexisting condition; not aggravated by service

What it means

VA found the condition began before you entered service. If it was not noted on your entrance exam, VA presumes you were sound, and VA must show by clear and unmistakable evidence both that it existed before service and that service did not aggravate it. If it was noted, the question is whether it got worse in service beyond its natural progress.

A next step that may fit

  • Look at your entrance exam. Whether the condition was written down there changes the rules.
  • Evidence that would matter: records or statements showing it got worse during service, and a medical opinion on aggravation.
  • See Existed before service.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 1111; 38 U.S.C. § 1153; 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(b); 38 C.F.R. § 3.306(a), (b) Court decision Wagner v. Principi, 370 F.3d 1089 (Fed. Cir. 2004)

not a disability for VA compensation purposesA condition the law does not compensate by itself

You may also see: congenital or developmental defect; refractive error

What it means

Some conditions are not "diseases or injuries" under VA's rules: congenital or developmental defects, refractive error of the eye (such as nearsightedness), and personality disorders. They cannot be service connected by themselves.

A next step that may fit

  • A disease (as opposed to a defect) that began in or was worsened by service can still be service connected, and so can a new disability added on top of a defect during service. Ask whether the letter treated your condition as a defect or a disease.
  • Evidence that would matter: a medical opinion explaining whether the condition is a disease and whether service added to it.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(c); 38 C.F.R. § 4.9; 38 C.F.R. § 4.127 VA guidance VA General Counsel opinions 82-90 and 67-90 (defect vs. disease)

failed to report for a VA examinationThe exam did not happen

You may also see: did not attend the exam; without good cause

What it means

If you miss a required exam without good cause, VA decides an original compensation claim on the evidence it has, which often leads to a denial. A claim for increase or a Supplemental Claim is denied. Good cause includes illness, hospitalization, or a death in the immediate family.

A next step that may fit

  • If you had a good reason, or never got the exam notice (for example, it went to an old address), tell VA, and say you are willing to report. Update your address on VA.gov.
  • A Supplemental Claim guide (20-0995) can restart the claim if a new exam is what is needed. See Lesson 4: Your C&P exam.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.655(a), (b)

no new and relevant evidenceA Supplemental Claim was not reopened

You may also see: insufficient evidence to readjudicate; not new and relevant

What it means

On a Supplemental Claim, VA decides the claim again only if it gets evidence that is new (not already in the record) and relevant (tends to prove or disprove a matter at issue, or raises a new theory). VA found nothing that met that bar, so it did not look at the claim again on its merits.

A next step that may fit

  • Evidence that would matter: evidence aimed at the specific element the earlier denial named. A copy of a record VA already had is not new.
  • A new theory, such as secondary or presumptive service connection, can count as relevant.
  • See Supplemental Claims and new exams and the Supplemental Claim guide (20-0995).

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5108; 38 C.F.R. § 3.2501(a), (b)

The presumption does not applyA presumption did not fit

You may also see: not a presumptive condition; not within the presumptive period

What it means

Presumptions remove the need to prove a link, but only for listed conditions, for qualifying service, and sometimes within a set time after service. VA found one of those requirements was not met.

A next step that may fit

  • A failed presumption does not end the claim. VA must still consider direct service connection with a medical link.
  • Check the dates and places of service in the letter against your records. See Presumptive service connection and PACT Act.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 1112; 38 U.S.C. § 1116; 38 C.F.R. § 3.307; 38 C.F.R. § 3.309; 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(d) Court decision Combee v. Brown, 34 F.3d 1039 (Fed. Cir. 1994)

Rating phrases

What the words around your percentage mean.

continuedYour rating stayed the same

You may also see: evaluation is continued; remains at

What it means

You asked for a higher rating (or VA reviewed the condition) and VA kept the same percentage. The letter should say what the next higher rating requires.

A next step that may fit

  • Compare your symptoms with the next higher rating's criteria quoted in the letter. That is the gap.
  • Evidence that would matter: treatment records or a statement showing symptoms that match the higher criteria, including flare-ups and how the condition limits work and daily life.
  • If the exam left out flare-ups or functional loss, see Pain and motion.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 4.1; 38 C.F.R. § 4.7; 38 C.F.R. § 3.103(f)(6)

A higher evaluation of … is not warranted unlessWhat the next level needs

You may also see: The next higher evaluation requires

What it means

This sentence quotes the rating schedule for the next percentage up. When symptoms fall between two levels, VA assigns the higher one if the picture more nearly approximates it.

A next step that may fit

  • Treat it as a checklist. Which of those symptoms do you have, and do your records show them?
  • VA must consider all of your symptoms, not only the ones listed, for some conditions such as mental health.
  • See Lesson 5: How ratings work.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 4.7; 38 C.F.R. § 4.21 Court decision Mauerhan v. Principi, 16 Vet. App. 436 (2002) (mental health symptoms are examples)

from … to …, and … from …Different percentages for different periods

You may also see: staged

What it means

VA can assign different ratings for different stretches of time when the evidence shows the condition changed. This is called a staged rating.

A next step that may fit

  • Check each date range against your records. If you were worse earlier than VA found, records from that period would matter.

Court decision Fenderson v. West, 12 Vet. App. 119 (1999); Hart v. Mansfield, 21 Vet. App. 505 (2007)

Combined evaluationWhy the total is not a simple sum

You may also see: Combined rating; bilateral factor

What it means

VA combines ratings with a table that applies each rating to the "remaining" efficiency, then rounds to the nearest 10. So 50% and 30% combine to 70%, not 80%. When both arms or both legs (or paired muscles) are rated, a bilateral factor of 10% of their combined value is added first.

A next step that may fit

  • Check the math with Combine ratings.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 4.25; 38 C.F.R. § 4.26

future examinationVA may check the condition again

You may also see: routine future exam; not static

What it means

VA may schedule a reexamination if the condition is likely to improve. A future exam is not a reduction. A rating can only be reduced after VA proposes it and gives you notice and time to respond.

A next step that may fit

  • Keep treatment records current so a later exam reflects your whole history.
  • If you get a proposal to reduce, deadlines are short. See Proposed reductions.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.327; 38 C.F.R. § 3.105(e), (i); 38 C.F.R. § 3.344

DeferredNot decided yet

You may also see: deferred pending

What it means

VA has not decided this issue yet, usually because it is waiting for an exam, an opinion, or records. A deferred issue is still pending, so there is nothing to appeal for it yet.

A next step that may fit

  • Watch for a later decision on that issue. Its review window starts from that later notice.
  • Answer any VA request for information promptly.

VA guidance M21-1, Part V, Subpart iv, Chapter 1 (rating decision format) Background "Deferred" is a VA decision-writing practice, not a term defined in the statute or regulations.

permanent and totalRated total and not expected to improve

You may also see: P&T; no future exams

What it means

VA found your disability totally disabling and reasonably certain to continue for life. Some family benefits, such as Chapter 35 education and CHAMPVA, depend on this finding.

A next step that may fit

  • See Chapter 35 education and CHAMPVA.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.340(a), (b); 38 U.S.C. § 3501

4 phrases

Dates, doubt, and what comes next

When payments start, how close calls are decided, and how to keep your date.

Effective dateThe date benefits start counting from

You may also see: We granted … effective

What it means

Usually the later of the date VA received your claim (or intent to file) and the date the condition began or met the criteria. If you claimed within one year after leaving service, it can be the day after discharge. Payment starts the first day of the month after the effective date.

A next step that may fit

  • Compare the date with your intent to file, your claim, and any earlier claim that was continuously pursued.
  • An earlier date is worth asking about if VA overlooked an earlier filing. See Effective dates and the Continuous Pursuit Review tool.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5110(a), (b)(1); 38 U.S.C. § 5111(a); 38 C.F.R. § 3.400(b)(2)(i); 38 C.F.R. § 3.155(b)

date entitlement aroseWhy VA picked a date later than your claim

You may also see: the date the evidence first showed

What it means

If the evidence shows the condition met the criteria only after your claim arrived, VA uses that later date. For an increase, VA can go back up to one year before the claim if records show the increase happened then.

A next step that may fit

  • Records from before the date VA picked, showing the same symptoms, would matter.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5110(b)(3); 38 C.F.R. § 3.400(o)(2)

benefit of the doubtTies go to the veteran

You may also see: reasonable doubt; approximate balance

What it means

When the evidence for and against an issue is in approximate balance, VA must resolve the doubt in your favor. It does not apply when the evidence clearly weighs against the claim.

A next step that may fit

  • If the letter says the evidence was against you, look for whether it explains why the opposing evidence carried more weight.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5107(b); 38 C.F.R. § 3.102 Court decision Gilbert v. Derwinski, 1 Vet. App. 49 (1990); Lynch v. McDonough, 21 F.4th 776 (Fed. Cir. 2021) (en banc)

This decision becomes finalYour window to ask for review

You may also see: within one year from the date of this letter

What it means

You generally have one year from the date of the notice to file a Supplemental Claim, Higher-Level Review, or Board appeal and keep the original effective date. After that, a Supplemental Claim can still be filed, but the effective date generally can be no earlier than when VA receives it.

A next step that may fit

  • Write the deadline down the day the letter arrives. Use the date on the letter, not the date you opened it.
  • Track your filings with the Continuous Pursuit Review tool.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.2500(a), (h)(1)–(2); 38 C.F.R. § 3.2501

Common mistakes

What often goes wrong after a decision

Reading only the first page.
The percentage is on page one, but the reasons and favorable findings, which tell you what is missing, come later.
Sending the same records again.
A copy of what VA already had is not new evidence. Aim new evidence at the element the denial named.
Ignoring the favorable findings.
They are already decided in your favor. Building evidence for something already proven wastes time.
Missing the one-year window.
Filing within a year of the notice generally keeps your original effective date.
Treating "deferred" as "denied."
A deferred issue is still pending. Wait for its decision.
Paying someone to file a first claim.
It is illegal to charge for help preparing an initial claim. Accredited VSOs help for free at every stage.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5904(c)(1); 38 C.F.R. § 3.104(c); 38 C.F.R. § 3.2500(h); 38 C.F.R. § 3.2501
