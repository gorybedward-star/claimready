Supplemental Claims: what new evidence does, and when VA orders a new exam

A Supplemental Claim asks VA to decide a claim again because you have new and relevant evidence. It works best when the new evidence speaks directly to the reason VA gave for its last decision. This article explains what counts as new and relevant, how to match your evidence to the reason for the denial, when VA's duty to help includes a new exam, and how to keep your effective date.

Based on public VA information: the law at 38 U.S.C. §§ 5103A and 5108 and 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.159 and 3.2501, VA's procedures manual (M21-1), and published court decisions. For education only. ClaimReady checks these sources for changes every week. Sources and dates

Start here

Supplemental Claims in five points

  • What it is. A request on VA Form 20-0995 for VA to decide again a benefit it already decided, using new and relevant evidence.
  • When you can file. Any time after VA sends you a decision. Filing within one year of the decision letter can protect your original effective date.
  • The bar is not high. The law says "new and relevant" is not a higher standard than the old "new and material" rule. The evidence does not have to win the claim; it has to bear on something VA must decide.
  • VA must help. Once VA receives a substantially complete Supplemental Claim, its duty to assist applies again, including getting the records you identify.
  • A new exam is not automatic. VA owes a new exam or opinion only after new and relevant evidence is in, and only when it is needed to decide the claim.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5108; 38 C.F.R. § 3.2501, (a), (c); § 3.159(c)(4)(iv) VA guidance M21-1 II.iii.2.B

The key test

What "new and relevant" means

New

  • Evidence that was not part of the record VA had when it made the earlier decision.
  • Sending the same records again does not count, even with a new cover letter.

Relevant

  • Evidence that tends to prove or disprove something VA has to decide.
  • That includes a theory VA has not addressed before. If direct service connection was denied, asking for secondary service connection is itself a new theory.

To be complete, the form only has to include or identify the new evidence. If you tell VA where your new records are, VA must try to get them.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.2501(a); § 3.159(c) VA guidance M21-1 II.iii.2.B.1; X.ii.2.A.2

Why did VA deny it, or what do you disagree with?

Your decision letter names the reason, often under "Reasons for decision." Pick the one that fits to see only that part. Nothing you pick is saved.

Showing every reason.

Reason: no current diagnosis

When VA found no current disability

Service connection starts with a current disability. If VA found none, new evidence should show one.

Evidence that speaks to it

  • A diagnosis from a doctor, in treatment records or on a Disability Benefits Questionnaire (DBQ).
  • Recent treatment records, test results, or imaging.
  • Your own detailed description of persistent or recurring symptoms: what they are, how often, and since when.

Will VA order a new exam?

  • VA owes one when the record shows a current disability or persistent or recurrent symptoms, something in service, and an indication the two may be linked, and does not have enough medical evidence to decide.
  • Lay evidence counts for the symptoms part. You do not need a diagnosis before VA examines you.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)(2); 38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)(4)(i)(A)

Reason: nothing in service

When VA found no event, injury, or disease in service

Evidence that speaks to it

  • Service treatment or personnel records VA did not have, or that you can point to.
  • A buddy statement (VA Form 21-10210) from someone who saw the injury or your symptoms in service.
  • Your own statement with the who, what, when, and where. A credible statement that fits your service can show an event even when records are silent.
  • For combat veterans, the law accepts a satisfactory account of an injury or disease in combat if it is consistent with that service.

Will VA order a new exam?

  • If the new evidence now shows something in service, and the record has a current disability or recurring symptoms and an indication of a link, VA generally owes an exam or opinion.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 1154(b); 38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)(4)(i)(B) VA guidance M21-1 V.ii.1.B.2

Reason: no link to service

When a negative opinion decided the claim

This is the most common reason for a Supplemental Claim. The new evidence should answer the specific reasoning in the opinion VA relied on.

Evidence that speaks to it

  • A private medical opinion that gives its reasons and addresses why the VA opinion's reasoning does or does not hold up. See What makes a medical opinion count.
  • Medical literature that supports a link, explained by a clinician in terms of your history.
  • Statements about symptoms that continued from service until now.
  • A different theory, such as secondary service connection.

Will VA order a new exam?

  • Not automatically. VA reviews a private favorable opinion for its rationale. If it is adequate, VA can weigh it against the earlier opinion and decide.
  • If the new evidence raises the question but is not enough to decide, VA can ask for a new opinion.
  • If you think the earlier VA opinion was inadequate and you have no new evidence, a Higher-Level Review can look for that duty to assist error instead.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)(4); § 3.2601(g) VA guidance M21-1 X.ii.2.A.2

Theory: secondary

When another condition caused it or made it worse

What VA needs

  • A condition that is already service connected, which stands in for the event in service.
  • Evidence that the claimed condition was caused by it, or has been made worse by it (aggravation). Both are separate ways to qualify.
  • Raising secondary for the first time is a new theory, so it is relevant by itself.

Will VA order a new exam?

  • The exam rule includes an indication that the condition may be associated with a service-connected disability.
  • A specific statement (for example, stomach problems since starting a medication for a rated condition), a medical treatise, or a VA rule linking the two can be enough to show a possible link.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(a)–(b); § 3.159(c)(4)(i)(C) VA guidance M21-1 IV.i.1.B.1 Court Allen v. Brown, 7 Vet. App. 439 (1995)

Disagreeing with the rating

When the percentage seems too low

Evidence that speaks to it

  • Records or a DBQ that show findings matching the next higher level of the rating criteria. Read what that level requires first.
  • Statements about flare-ups, frequency, and how the condition limits work and daily life.
  • Evidence that a related benefit applies, such as special monthly compensation or individual unemployability.

Will VA order a new exam?

  • VA's guidance: an exam is ordered when the new evidence shows a picture that does not match the current rating. VA's example is a back rated 10% for pain with full motion, where new records show only treatment for pain: no new exam.
  • If the condition is already at the highest rating its diagnostic code allows, VA can decide without an exam. Other benefits may still be worth asking about.

If your condition has gotten worse since the decision, a new claim for an increase is also possible. ClaimReady note, from general knowledge: VA's public site directs increase requests to VA Form 21-526EZ.

VA guidance M21-1 X.ii.2.A.2; IV.i.1.B.1

Reason: missed exam

When VA denied it after a missed exam

  • If you miss an exam without good cause on a Supplemental Claim, the law says the claim is denied.
  • VA's guidance says that after the decision, telling VA you are willing to attend counts as new and relevant evidence, and VA will order the exam.
  • VA generally accepts any reasonable explanation, such as illness, hospitalization, or a family emergency. Give it as soon as you can.
  • Keep your address and phone number current with VA. Not answering scheduling calls and letters can count as missing the exam.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 3.655 VA guidance M21-1 IV.i.2.F.1

Reason: never examined

When VA decided without ever examining you

VA must provide an exam or opinion when it is needed to decide a compensation claim. A court case, McLendon v. Nicholson, explains the test VA uses. All four parts must be met:

  1. A current disability, or persistent or recurrent symptoms of one.Your own description of symptoms counts.
  2. Something in service: an event, injury, or disease, or a presumptive condition in its time window.
  3. An indication they may be linked.A low bar, lower than what it takes to win. A credible statement that symptoms have continued since service can meet it. A bare "it's related" may not.
  4. Not enough medical evidence to decide without an exam.
  • On a Supplemental Claim, this test applies after new and relevant evidence is in the record.
  • If you believe the record already met the test when VA decided, a Higher-Level Review can treat the missing exam as a duty to assist error and send it back.
  • Toxic exposure claims have their own exam rule. See Toxic exposure exams and opinions (TERA).

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d); § 1168; 38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)(4) Court McLendon v. Nicholson, 20 Vet. App. 79 (2006); Waters v. Shinseki, 601 F.3d 1274 (Fed. Cir. 2010)

After you file

How VA handles a Supplemental Claim, in plain steps

  1. VA checks that the form is complete.It needs the issue and new evidence included or identified. If incomplete, VA tells you; finishing it within 60 days keeps the filing date.
  2. VA gets the evidence you pointed to.Private records need your authorization. VA treatment records you name are requested.
  3. VA decides whether the evidence is new and relevant.If not, you get a decision saying there is not enough to decide again. You can seek review of that.
  4. If it is, VA decides whether an exam or opinion is needed.Using the test above, or its guidance for rating disagreements.
  5. VA decides the claim again on all the evidence.Findings already made in your favor stay in place unless clear and unmistakable evidence shows they were wrong.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5104A; 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.104(c), 3.155, 3.2501 VA guidance M21-1 X.ii.2.A.1–2

Example

How this might look for Dana

Fictional example

VA denied Dana's hearing loss claim. The VA opinion said hearing was normal at separation, so there was no link to flight line noise. Two months later, Dana filed a Supplemental Claim with a private audiologist's opinion.

  • New: the private opinion was not in the record before.
  • Relevant: it speaks to the reason for the denial, the link to service. The audiologist explained, with reasons, why a normal hearing test at separation does not by itself rule out noise-related hearing loss later.
  • Exam: VA reviews the private opinion's rationale. VA might weigh the two opinions and decide, or ask for a new VA opinion.
  • Date: Dana filed within one year, so a grant could reach back to the original claim date.

This is a made-up example to show how the rules fit together. It is not a real VA decision and does not predict any outcome.

Common misunderstandings

What people often get wrong

"Sending my records again counts."
No. Evidence already in the record is not new.
"The new evidence has to prove my case."
No. It has to tend to prove or disprove something at issue. VA then weighs all the evidence.
"VA will always give me a new exam."
No. VA decides whether one is needed after the new evidence comes in.
"After a year, I can't file."
You can file any time. After a year, the effective date is usually later.
"A new doctor's letter can go with a Higher-Level Review."
No. A Higher-Level Review cannot consider new evidence. A new opinion goes with a Supplemental Claim.
