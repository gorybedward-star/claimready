Somatic symptom disorder, ADHD and other neurodevelopmental disorders, and insomnia: how VA decides service connection

These three groups of conditions are handled differently from most mental health claims. Somatic symptom disorder is accepted as a disability and rated on what it actually causes. Neurodevelopmental disorders such as ADHD and autism generally cannot be service connected directly, with two exceptions. Insomnia is usually treated as a symptom of another condition, and only rarely as its own rated disability. This article explains each rule, the exceptions, and what the medical evidence needs to show.

Part 1

Somatic symptom disorder

Somatic symptom disorder involves physical symptoms, such as pain, along with distress or excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviors about those symptoms. VA's manual says it is widely recognized as disabling and is accepted as a disability for compensation purposes.

What VA's manual says

  • It can come from an underlying disease, such as multiple sclerosis or arthritis.
  • Variations of it can show up in every body system.
  • So VA rates it under the most appropriate diagnostic code for the clinical picture, which may be a mental disorder code or a physical one.
  • VA already recognizes fibromyalgia and low back pain syndrome, which the manual calls forms of somatic symptom disorder.
  • DSM-5 replaced the older diagnosis of chronic pain syndrome with somatic symptom disorder.

What the evidence needs to show

VA's manual says adequate medical evidence must identify the specific manifestations present. A diagnosis alone is not enough to rate it accurately.

  • Which symptoms you have, and where in the body
  • How often and how severe they are
  • How they affect work, daily activities, and relationships
  • Any underlying disease the symptoms come from

The mental disorder part of the rating schedule lists somatic symptom disorder as diagnostic code 9421, with related codes 9422 to 9425 (other specified and unspecified somatic symptom disorders, conversion disorder, and illness anxiety disorder). Those codes use the General Rating Formula for Mental Disorders, which looks at occupational and social impairment.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 4.130, DCs 9421–9425 VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.13.1.i

Part 1, continued

When one condition has physical and mental symptoms

Because somatic symptom disorder sits between the body and the mind, the same symptoms can look like a physical condition or a mental one. The rating rules prevent counting them twice.

  • Rate the dominant part. When a single disability is diagnosed both as a physical condition and as a mental disorder, VA uses the code for the dominant, more disabling aspect.
  • No double counting. The same disability, or the same symptoms, cannot be rated twice under different diagnoses. This rule is called the rule against pyramiding.
  • Separate problems can still be rated separately. If a physical condition and a mental condition cause different symptoms, each can have its own rating.

Law 38 C.F.R. §§ 4.14, 4.126(d) VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.13.1.h (referenced in 13.1.i)

Part 2

ADHD, autism, and other neurodevelopmental disorders

Neurodevelopmental disorders begin during the developmental period. DSM-5 describes them as typically showing up early in life, with developmental deficits in several areas of functioning. VA's manual lists these as examples, but the group is not limited to them:

  • Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD, also called ADD)
  • Autism spectrum disorder
  • Specific learning disorder
  • Tic disorder
  • Childhood-onset fluency disorder (stuttering)
  • Intellectual developmental disorder (intellectual disability)

VA treats neurodevelopmental disorders as not "diseases or injuries" under 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(c), so they are generally not service connected on a direct basis.

The regulation itself says congenital or developmental defects, personality disorders, and mental deficiency are not diseases or injuries for compensation purposes. VA's manual applies that rule to the whole group of neurodevelopmental disorders. A separate regulation, § 4.127, says the same for intellectual disability specifically.

Law 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.303(c), 4.127 VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.13.1.j

Part 2, continued

Two exceptions in VA's manual

"Generally not" is not "never." VA's manual names two situations where service connection should be considered.

  1. An in-service injury caused the diagnosis. If the evidence clearly shows the diagnosis developed because of an injury in service, such as a traumatic brain injury (TBI), VA considers service connection for any diagnosis directly related to that injury.Example: attention and concentration problems that first appear after a documented head injury in service, with a medical opinion linking them to the TBI.
  2. The condition worsened at an abnormally high rate during service. If it progressed faster than its normal course while you were in service, VA considers service connection by aggravation under 38 C.F.R. § 3.306.Under § 3.306, a preexisting condition that increases in severity during service is presumed aggravated unless the increase is shown to be its natural progress.

The presumption of soundness

By law, a veteran is presumed to have been in sound condition at entry, except for conditions noted on the entry exam. VA can rebut that only with clear and unmistakable evidence that the condition existed before service and was not aggravated by it.

VA's manual points to Horn v. Shinseki (2012) for this topic. Board decisions cite Horn for the two parts of that test, called the preexistence prong and the aggravation prong, and for the meaning of "clear and unmistakable": evidence that is undebatable.

What to look for in your records

  • Was the condition noted on your entry exam, or not?
  • Is there a documented head injury or other injury in service, and did new symptoms follow it?
  • Do service records show the condition getting much worse, such as new treatment, duty limits, or a medical board?
  • Does a doctor explain whether the change was faster than the condition's normal course?

Part 2, continued

A separate condition on top of a neurodevelopmental disorder

Even when the neurodevelopmental disorder itself cannot be service connected, a different condition that develops in or because of service is its own claim.

  • For intellectual disability, the regulation says disability from a mental disorder superimposed on it may be service connected.
  • A veteran with ADHD who develops depression or PTSD from service can claim the depression or PTSD. The ADHD diagnosis does not block it.
  • A TBI, PTSD, or other service-connected condition is evaluated on its own symptoms, so the record should separate which symptoms come from which condition where a doctor can do that.
From ClaimReady's research, not the M21-1 text

Board decisions also rely on VA General Counsel opinions (VAOPGCPREC 82-90 and 67-90) that separate congenital or developmental defects, which are generally stable, from diseases, which can improve or worsen, and say a defect can be subject to a superimposed disease or injury. VA's manual does not cite those opinions in this section.

Part 3

Insomnia: a symptom or its own condition?

VA's manual says to consider the evidence carefully. Insomnia is generally a symptom of another disability. It can occur on its own, but it is often part of:

Another mental disorder

For example, major depressive disorder or PTSD

A medical condition

For example, pain that keeps you awake

Another sleep disorder

For example, a breathing-related sleep disorder such as sleep apnea

Chronic sleep impairment is already one of the symptoms listed in the General Rating Formula for Mental Disorders, at the 30% level. That is one reason sleep problems caused by a mental disorder are counted inside that disorder's rating.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 4.130 VA guidance M21-1 V.iii.13.1.k

Part 3, continued

The two paths for insomnia

VA's manual uses a simple table. Which path applies depends on whether something else causes the insomnia.

Path A · If

Insomnia is caused by, or is a symptom of, another service-connected condition

  • No separate rating for insomnia on a secondary basis.
  • VA service connects the underlying condition, if it is not already.
  • The insomnia symptoms are included in the rating for that underlying condition.
  • The decision should explain that insomnia was counted there because that condition causes it.
  • No rating under a mental disorder code just for insomnia, unless the condition causing it is another mental disorder with a DSM-5 diagnosis.
Path B · If all three are true

Insomnia disorder on its own, linked to service

  • A current DSM-5 diagnosis of insomnia disorder, meaning it is not tied to another disease or injury
  • An event in service, such as a diagnosis of primary insomnia or insomnia disorder during service
  • A medical link (nexus) between today's diagnosis and that event

Then: service connection on a direct basis, rated by analogy under a mental disorder code in § 4.130, because the schedule has no code of its own for insomnia.

A separate rating for insomnia disorder is only warranted if all other possible causes are ruled out and it is service connected directly.

DSM-5 renamed "primary insomnia" to "insomnia disorder." VA's manual reads both versions as requiring that other causes be ruled out, so a valid diagnosis means the insomnia is not caused by, or secondary to, another condition.

Medical background, not from VA

DSM-5 says insomnia disorder is not adequately explained by coexisting conditions, and it also lets a clinician note insomnia disorder alongside another mental or medical condition when the insomnia needs attention on its own. So a medical record may list insomnia disorder next to depression or pain. How that fits VA's two paths is a question for the examiner's explanation and for VA. Ask your doctor to explain what is causing your insomnia.

What "rated by analogy" means

When a condition is not listed in the rating schedule, VA may rate it under a closely related condition where the functions affected, the part of the body, and the symptoms are closely analogous. The rule avoids guesswork analogies and analogous ratings for conditions of doubtful diagnosis or not fully supported by clinical findings. For insomnia disorder, that means a mental disorder code and the General Rating Formula.

Ties it together

Why the DSM-5 diagnosis matters

For any mental disorder, VA requires a diagnosis that follows DSM-5 and is supported by the exam findings. If it is not, the regulation says VA returns the report to the examiner to support the diagnosis. That rule is why the exact wording matters in all three parts of this article: a DSM-5 somatic symptom disorder, a neurodevelopmental diagnosis, and a DSM-5 insomnia disorder each lead to a different result.

Law 38 C.F.R. § 4.125(a)

For veterans, representatives, and decision makers

Questions to ask about a decision

  • Somatic symptom disorder: Does the medical evidence describe the specific symptoms, and does the code chosen fit that clinical picture? If the same condition has physical and mental symptoms, was the dominant part rated, without counting symptoms twice?
  • Neurodevelopmental disorder: If the record shows a head injury or other injury in service, or a sharp worsening in service, did the decision consider those exceptions? Was a separate mental disorder on top of it considered on its own?
  • Insomnia, Path A: If insomnia was not rated separately, does the decision say which condition it was counted in, and is that condition service connected?
  • Insomnia, Path B: Is there a DSM-5 diagnosis of insomnia disorder, an in-service event, and a medical opinion that explains the link and why other causes were ruled out?
  • Diagnosis: Does the exam give a DSM-5 diagnosis supported by its findings?

Examples

How this might look

Fictional example 1: insomnia

An Army veteran is service connected for PTSD and also claims insomnia. The examiner explains that the sleep problems are part of the PTSD.

  • This is Path A. No separate insomnia rating.
  • The sleep problems are counted in the PTSD rating under the General Rating Formula, and the decision explains that.
Fictional example 2: ADHD

A Marine veteran was diagnosed with ADHD as a child, and it was not noted at entry. In service, the veteran had a documented TBI from a blast, followed by new memory and concentration problems.

  • The ADHD itself is generally not service connected.
  • VA considers service connection for any diagnosis directly related to the TBI, and a medical opinion helps separate TBI symptoms from long-standing ADHD symptoms.

These are made-up examples to show how the rules fit together. They are not real VA decisions and do not predict any outcome.

Common misunderstandings

What people often get wrong

"Somatic symptom disorder isn't a real disability for VA."
VA's manual says it is accepted as a disability for compensation. It is rated under the code that best fits the symptoms.
"ADHD can never be connected to service."
Generally not directly, but VA considers it when an in-service injury caused the diagnosis or the condition worsened at an abnormally high rate in service. A separate mental disorder on top of it is its own claim.
"I should get a separate rating for insomnia on top of my PTSD rating."
Usually not. When another condition causes insomnia, the sleep problems are counted in that condition's rating.
"Insomnia can't be service connected at all."
It can, on a direct basis, when there is a DSM-5 insomnia disorder with no other cause, an event in service, and a medical link.
"Insomnia from back pain gets its own mental health rating."
VA's manual says no mental disorder code just for insomnia when it is secondary to another disability, unless that disability is itself a DSM-5 mental disorder.
