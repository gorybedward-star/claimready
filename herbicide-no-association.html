Not on the Agent Orange list? What "no positive association" really means

VA keeps a list of conditions it presumes were caused by herbicides such as Agent Orange. It also keeps a second, less known list: conditions the Secretary of Veterans Affairs looked at and decided do not get a presumption. If your condition is on that second list, your claim is not over. It means you need a different route, called direct service connection. This article explains both lists, what "no positive association" means in plain English, and what can help a direct claim.

Based on public sources: 38 U.S.C. §§ 1113, 1116, 1168, and 1174, 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.303, 3.307, and 3.309(e), the Secretary's notices in the Federal Register, published court decisions, and VA's procedures manual, M21-1, Part VIII, Subpart i, Chapter 1, Section B, Topic 1.g. Start here

No presumption is not the same as no claim

If VA says there is "no positive association" between herbicides and your condition, that only takes away the shortcut. You can still show, with evidence, that your condition is related to your service.

Congress wrote this into the law: nothing in the presumption statutes, including the Agent Orange law, "shall be construed to prevent the granting of service-connection for any disease or disorder otherwise shown by sound judgment to have been incurred in or aggravated by" service.

With a presumption

  • VA presumes the link between your exposure and your condition.
  • You need a qualifying place and dates of service, and a diagnosis (plus any time or severity rule).
  • You usually do not need a doctor to explain the link.

Without a presumption (direct)

  • You need the same three things as any direct claim: a current condition, an event or exposure in service, and a medical link between them.
  • If VA concedes herbicide exposure, the second element is usually met already.
  • The key evidence is a medical opinion about you, explaining why your exposure is at least as likely as not a cause.

Plain English

What "no positive association" means

The law used a specific test. A link between a disease and herbicide exposure counted as a positive association if "the credible evidence for the association is equal to or outweighs the credible evidence against the association." VA's manual states the reverse: no positive association means the evidence for a link does not equal or outweigh the evidence against it.

  1. It is about groups of people.The finding comes from scientific studies of large populations, reviewed by the National Academy of Sciences (now the National Academies) since 1993.
  2. It is mostly "not enough evidence."In the 2014 notice, most of these conditions were in the category "inadequate or insufficient evidence to determine an association." That is different from proof that herbicides cannot cause them.
  3. It is not a finding about you.The notice itself says the determination "does not in any way preclude VA from granting service connection for any disease."
Where these decisions come from, and why the lists change
  • The Agent Orange Act of 1991 had the Secretary review each National Academy of Sciences report and decide, for each condition, whether a presumption was warranted. When the answer was no, the Secretary published a notice in the Federal Register explaining the science. Notices like this appeared from 1994 through 2014.
  • That part of the law expired on September 30, 2015. The PACT Act (2022) replaced it with a general process for all toxic exposures. Under it, when the Secretary decides a new presumption is not warranted, the Secretary must still publish a notice in the Federal Register with the reasons.
  • Lists change. Conditions once listed as having no positive association have later been added as presumptions. Bladder cancer and hypothyroidism were added by Congress in 2021, and high blood pressure (hypertension) in 2022. Prostate cancer was denied a presumption in 1994, a decision a federal court upheld in 1995, and VA added it later.

The list

Conditions VA says have no positive association with herbicides

This is the list in VA's current manual, which draws on the Secretary's determinations. Watch the words "excluding": they point to conditions that are handled differently, usually because they are on the presumptive list.

Cancers

  • Bone and connective tissue
  • Brain and nervous system (including eye)
  • Digestive: esophageal, stomach, and colorectal (including small intestine and anus)
  • Endocrine (including thyroid and thymus)
  • Hepatobiliary (liver, gallbladder, and bile ducts) and pancreatic
  • Leukemia, excluding all chronic B-cell leukemias, including chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and hairy cell
  • Nasal cavity (including ears and sinuses)
  • Oral cavity (including lips and tongue)
  • Pharynx (including tonsils)
  • Pleura, mediastinum, and other unspecified sites within the respiratory system and intrathoracic organs
  • Skin (including melanoma and nonmelanoma: basal cell and squamous cell)
  • Reproductive (including the cervix, uterus, ovary, testes, breast, and penis), excluding prostate

Other conditions

  • Asthma
  • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
  • Circulatory disorders, excluding hypertension, ischemic heart disease (IHD), and stroke
  • Endometriosis
  • Eye diseases
  • Farmer's lung
  • Gastrointestinal, metabolic, and digestive disorders
  • Hearing loss
  • Immune system disorders (immune suppression, allergy, and autoimmunity)
  • Neurobehavioral (cognitive and neuropsychiatric) disorders
  • Neurodegenerative diseases (including ALS), excluding Parkinson's disease and parkinsonism
  • Osteoporosis
  • Chronic peripheral nervous system disorders, excluding early-onset peripheral neuropathy

Excluded does not always mean presumed. Most of the conditions after "excluding" are on the presumptive list. Stroke is the exception: it is not presumptive for herbicides, but it is also not on this list, so it is not treated as a "no association" condition.

Building the claim

What can help a direct claim

VA weighs all of the evidence, and when it is evenly balanced, the doubt goes to you. These are the pieces VA looks for.

  1. A current diagnosis, with its date. For a cancer, the primary site (where it started) and the type, from a pathology report if you have one.
  2. Your exposure. Proof of service in a place where VA presumes herbicide exposure, or other evidence that you were exposed. Describe your duties if they involved spraying, handling drums, or clearing vegetation.See Proving Vietnam service for how VA decides where you served.
  3. A medical opinion about you. The strongest opinions explain, in the doctor's own reasoning, why your exposure is "at least as likely as not" a cause, and they address your own history:
    • when and how the condition appeared;
    • other risk factors, such as smoking, family history, sun exposure, or work after service, and why they do or do not explain it better;
    • medical or scientific literature, including studies newer than the government's reviews.
  4. Everything else that fits. Treatment records, your own statement of symptoms and when they began, and statements from people who knew you.

A general article or study by itself is usually given less weight than an opinion that applies it to your facts. See ClaimReady's guides to medical opinions and talking with your doctor.

Examinations

Will VA order an exam?

Since the PACT Act, VA generally must give you a toxic exposure exam and medical opinion when you have a disability, evidence of a toxic exposure risk activity (TERA) in service, and not enough evidence yet to grant. The law has one exception: VA does not have to when it finds "no indication of an association" between the disability and the exposure.

  • VA guidance VA treats this list as one of those exceptions, but only when herbicides are your only established toxic exposure. So VA may decide a claim for a listed condition without ordering a toxic exposure opinion.
  • Your evidence can change that. VA's guidance says VA still considers an opinion when you submit competent medical or scientific evidence that suggests a link in your case, such as a doctor's letter.
  • Other exposures count. If you also had another TERA, such as burn pits or jet fuel, the herbicide exception does not cover that exposure.
  • VA's general duty to get an exam still applies whenever the evidence indicates your condition may be related to service. Courts have called that a low threshold.

Either way, if VA denies the claim, the decision must say which element was not met. If an exam was skipped because of an exception, the decision should say so.

Law 38 U.S.C. §§ 1168(a)-(c), 1710(e)(4), 5103A(d), 5104(b) Court McLendon v. Nicholson, 20 Vet. App. 79 (2006) VA guidance M21-1 VIII.i.1.B.1.g; VA's PACT Act claims guidance More on TERA exams

Reading a decision

If you were denied

Look closely at the reasons. Two problems come up often with conditions on this list.

Only the presumption was addressed

If the decision explains why the condition is not presumptive but never discusses a direct link, it may not have done the full analysis. The Federal Circuit held that when a presumption does not apply, VA must still review the claim on a direct basis.

The opinion says "not on VA's list"

The Veterans Court has found opinions inadequate when they rest only on the fact that VA does not presume the link, or only on the condition's category in the national science reviews, without looking at the veteran's own risk factors and history.

Your options include a Higher-Level Review, a Supplemental Claim with new and relevant evidence (such as a new medical opinion), or an appeal to the Board. Deadlines apply. See Supplemental Claims, and talk with an accredited representative, who can help at no cost.

Court Combee v. Brown, 34 F.3d 1039, 1043-44 (Fed. Cir. 1994); Stefl v. Nicholson, 21 Vet. App. 120, 123-24 (2007); Polovick v. Shinseki, 23 Vet. App. 48, 52-55 (2009) Law 38 U.S.C. §§ 5104B, 5108, 7105

Example

How this might look

Fictional example

An Army veteran served in Vietnam in 1969 and was diagnosed with colon cancer in 2021. The veteran never smoked and has no family history of colon cancer.

  • Exposure: service in Vietnam is shown in the records, so herbicide exposure is presumed.
  • The presumption: colorectal cancer is on the "no positive association" list, so VA does not presume the link.
  • The exam: herbicides are the veteran's only established exposure, so VA might not order a toxic exposure opinion on its own.
  • What could help: the veteran's oncologist writes a letter that reviews the veteran's history and risk factors, cites the literature, and explains the doctor's reasoning on whether the cancer is at least as likely as not related to the exposure. VA then weighs that letter with the rest of the record, and could order its own opinion.

This is a made-up example to show how the rules fit together. It is not a real VA decision and does not predict any outcome.

Common misunderstandings

What people often get wrong

"VA says Agent Orange doesn't cause my condition."
Not quite. The finding is that the evidence across large groups of people was not strong enough for a presumption. For most of these conditions, the science reviews called the evidence inadequate or insufficient, not proof of no link.
"If it's not presumptive, there's no point filing."
The law says presumptions do not prevent service connection shown by the evidence. Your claim is decided on a direct basis.
"VA has to give me an exam for every condition."
Not always. For a condition on this list, with herbicides as your only exposure, VA's guidance may not require a toxic exposure opinion. Submitting your own medical evidence can change that.
"A study online proves my case."
A study can help, but VA usually gives more weight to a doctor who applies the research to your own history.
"My cancer spread to my lungs, so it's presumed."
Not if it started somewhere else. VA looks at the primary site.
"The list will never change."
It has changed many times. Bladder cancer, hypothyroidism, and high blood pressure were all once on the "no association" side.
