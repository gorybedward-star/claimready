What VA asks your examiner

For some kinds of claims, VA adds extra questions to the exam request it sends to the examiner. VA publishes that wording. This guide explains it in plain English, so you know what the examiner is asked to cover, what to bring, and what to look for in the report. You can also share it with your own doctor.

Based on public VA guidance: the Supplemental Language Matrix (KnowVA, updated Apr 9, 2026) and M21-1, Part IV, Subpart i, Chapter 2, Section A (updated Aug 18, 2026). ClaimReady checks both for changes every week. Sources and dates

Every exam

Three things to know first

  • The exam gathers evidence. It is not treatment. VA also does not order an exam just to make a diagnosis that would need sedation or an invasive procedure, such as a biopsy. If that kind of diagnosis is needed, records from your own treatment matter.
  • Who examines you depends on the condition. Hearing, vision, dental, and psychiatric exams must be done by a specialist. Most other exams are done by a clinician who is not a specialist, using the form (DBQ) for the claimed condition.
  • You have no legal right to bring a lawyer into the exam or to record it.

For your rights, exam day, and what a complete report needs, see C&P exams: the complete guide.

VA guidance M21-1 IV.i.2.A.1.d–h

Joints, spine, muscles, and jaw

If medicine helps: how bad is it without it?

When you take medicine for a joint, spine, muscle, bone, or jaw condition, VA asks the examiner to estimate how severe the condition would be without the medicine (VA calls this the "unmedicated state"), when that can be done without guessing.

Which exam forms this covers (16)
  • Amputations
  • Ankle
  • Non-degenerative arthritis and decompression illness
  • Back (thoracolumbar spine)
  • Bones and other skeletal conditions
  • Elbow and forearm
  • Foot, including flatfoot
  • Hand and fingers
  • Hip and thigh
  • Knee and lower leg
  • Muscle injuries
  • Neck (cervical spine)
  • Osteomyelitis
  • Shoulder and arm
  • Jaw (TMD)
  • Wrist

The questions the examiner is asked

  1. Do you take any medicine for this condition? Prescription or over the counter.If no, the questions stop here.
  2. Does it relieve or reduce your symptoms?If no, the questions stop here.
  3. Can the examiner estimate the severity as if you were not taking it, without resorting to mere speculation?If no, the examiner must explain why in the report. VA's wording says the reason should not be the examiner's own shortcomings or a general reluctance to estimate something not seen in the room.
  4. If yes, which kinds of functional loss would be worse, and by how much? The examiner checks each that applies and estimates the added loss, ideally as added loss of motion in degrees:
    • Pain
    • Active range of motion
    • Passive range of motion
    • Repetitive use
    • Repeated use over time
    • Flare-ups
    • Ankylosis (a joint fixed in place)
    • Flail joints
    • Other factors, such as trouble standing or sitting, walking problems, weakness, instability, swelling, deformity, or muscle wasting from disuse

Nerve symptoms from the spine are different. VA is not required to consider nerve problems from the spine, such as radiculopathy, in an unmedicated state, so the examiner skips these questions for them on the neck and back forms.

Fictional exampleDana takes a daily prescription anti-inflammatory for a service-connected knee. At the exam, Dana explains that it takes the edge off the pain, and that on days a dose was missed, Dana could not kneel or handle stairs. That history gives the examiner something concrete to base an estimate on, or to explain why an estimate is not possible. This is an illustration, not a VA decision.

Before your exam

  • Bring a list of every medicine you take for the condition, prescription and over the counter, with the dose and how often.
  • Be ready to say how much each one helps, and what the condition is like when a dose wears off or is missed.
  • Do not stop or change a medicine for an exam. Talk to the doctor who prescribed it about any change.
  • Afterward, check the report. If you said a medicine helps, look for an estimate or an explanation of why one was not possible.

For your own doctor

  • If you complete one of these forms for the veteran, the same four questions apply.
  • Give the estimate as added loss of motion in degrees where you can, with your reasoning.
  • If an estimate is not possible, explain why in a way tied to the facts, not a general reluctance.

Claims for increase

If your condition got worse, or got a new name

For an increase, the examiner is asked to evaluate how severe your service-connected condition is now.

Sometimes the examiner's diagnosis is different from the one VA service connected. VA's wording then asks the examiner to say which of these is true:

  • The new diagnosis is a progression of the service-connected condition.For example, a condition that developed or changed over time.
  • The original diagnosis was in error.Only with a rationale, supported by the clinical evidence in the record, that refutes the earlier exam or exams that made the diagnosis.

"If the diagnosis was in error, please provide a rationale supported by the clinical evidence of record that refutes the previous exam(s) which diagnosed the condition."

Before your exam

  • Be ready to describe what has changed since your last rating: new symptoms, more frequent flare-ups, more treatment, work or daily tasks you can no longer do.
  • Bring or submit recent treatment records, especially any that use a different name for the condition.
  • If the report calls the old diagnosis an error, check that it explains why, using the record.

For your own doctor

  • Describe current severity in the terms of the matching exam form.
  • If your diagnosis differs from the rated one, say whether it is a progression of that condition, and why.

Gulf War and Southwest Asia service

Symptoms without a clear cause

VA's laws allow service connection for certain chronic disability patterns linked to environmental hazards in Southwest Asia. VA gives the examiner definitions so the report can say which pattern fits.

Hazards VA's wording names
  • Smoke and particles from oil well fires
  • Pesticides and insecticides
  • Infectious diseases found in the region
  • Solvent and fuel fumes
  • Pyridostigmine bromide tablets, taken as a nerve gas antidote
  • The combined effect of multiple vaccines given at deployment
  • Very fine sand particles
  • Smoke and particles from burn pits

The four patterns

  1. Undiagnosed illness. Findings that cannot be attributed to a known, clearly defined diagnosis after likely diagnoses have been ruled out.
  2. Diagnosed but medically unexplained chronic multisymptom illness (MUCMI). A diagnosis with no conclusive cause or mechanism, with overlapping symptoms such as fatigue, pain, disability out of proportion to physical findings, and inconsistent lab results.Examples include chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, and irritable bowel syndrome.
  3. Chronic multisymptom illness with a partially explained cause.VA's examples: diabetes and multiple sclerosis. These are not MUCMIs.
  4. A disease with a clear and specific cause and diagnosis.

Patterns 1 and 2 (shaded) are the ones the Gulf War presumption covers. Patterns 3 and 4 can still be service connected in other ways, such as direct service connection or another presumption. VA decides which pattern applies based on the whole record.

Chronic means

Lasting 6 months or more, or coming and going over a 6-month period. The 6 months count from the earliest date the evidence shows the symptoms began.

Objective indications

Signs an examiner can observe, and also non-medical indicators that can be independently verified.

Signs and symptoms that may qualify

  • Fatigue
  • Skin
  • Headache
  • Muscle pain
  • Joint pain
  • Neurological
  • Neuropsychological
  • Upper or lower respiratory
  • Sleep disturbances
  • Gastrointestinal
  • Cardiovascular
  • Abnormal weight loss
  • Menstrual disorders

Still in service? For claims filed before discharge (Benefits Delivery at Discharge or the Integrated Disability Evaluation System), VA's wording asks the examiner for a medical statement, with rationale, placing the disability pattern in one of the four groups. For pattern 1 or 2, the examiner identifies which signs and symptoms belong to each illness. For pattern 3 or 4, no further opinion is requested.

Before your exam

  • List every symptom, when it started, how often it happens, and how long it lasts.
  • Note any tests or treatment that did not find a cause.
  • Statements from people who saw your symptoms over time can help show they are chronic.

For your own doctor

  • Say which of the four patterns fits, with your reasoning.
  • If a diagnosis is on record, say whether it explains all of the symptoms, and name any it does not.
  • Note when symptoms began and whether they have lasted 6 months.

Digestive conditions

Functional or structural?

VA asks examiners to tell apart two kinds of digestive conditions. For Gulf War claims, it matters because functional disorders can be MUCMIs and structural diseases cannot.

Functional

Chronic or recurring symptoms that are not explained by structural, endoscopic, lab, or other objective signs of injury or disease.

Examples: irritable bowel syndrome, functional dyspepsia, functional vomiting, functional constipation, functional bloating, functional abdominal pain syndrome, and functional dysphagia.

Structural

A disease that testing does show, for example on imaging, endoscopy, or lab work.

VA's Gulf War guidance names GERD, ulcerative colitis, and Crohn's disease as structural (M21-1 VIII.ii.1.A.1.h).

Common symptoms of functional disorders include abdominal pain, burning or pain behind the breastbone, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea or constipation, indigestion, bloating, feeling full after eating, and painful or difficult swallowing. Diagnosis generally requires symptoms that began at least 6 months before diagnosis and were enough to diagnose the disorder at least 3 months before.

When a digestive disorder is found in a pre-discharge Gulf War exam, the examiner is asked to explain whether it is functional or structural, and to discuss the testing used to identify it.

Before your exam

  • Write down when your symptoms started and how often they happen.
  • Bring results of any scopes, imaging, or lab work, including tests that came back normal.

For your own doctor

  • Say whether the disorder is functional or structural, and which tests support that.

VA also has separate added wording for every digestive system exam form, published as an attachment to the matrix. This guide does not cover that attachment yet.

Changed rating rules

If VA changed the rating criteria while your claim was pending

VA sometimes rewrites the criteria for a group of conditions. When that happens while an initial or increased rating claim is pending, VA's guidance says the exam request for affected conditions should include wording for the old (historical) criteria too, and VA must consider the old criteria and the related exam results when it decides.

  • This also applies when the claim came in after the change, but an intent to file submitted before the change was still active.
  • Check your exam report: it should give the findings each version of the criteria needs.
  • Example: knee and hip replacement ratings changed on February 7, 2021. The Temporary 100% Timeline shows both versions.
