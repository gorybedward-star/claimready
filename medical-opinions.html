What makes a medical opinion count in a VA claim

Many VA claims turn on a medical opinion: is a condition at least as likely as not related to service? VA does not count opinions; it weighs them, and most of an opinion's weight comes from its reasoning. This article explains what VA and the courts look for, for veterans reading an exam report, doctors writing an opinion, and lawyers, agents, and service officers reviewing one.

Based on public VA information: the law at 38 U.S.C. §§ 5103A and 5107(b) and 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.102 and 3.159, VA's procedures manual (M21-1, Part V, Subpart ii, Chapter 1), and published court decisions. For education only. Sources and dates

What a medical opinion does

  • Service connection needs a current disability, something in service, and a link between them. A medical opinion most often speaks to the link. It can also speak to a diagnosis, when a condition began, whether it got worse, or how severe it is.
  • The doctor gives medical facts and reasons. VA makes the legal decision. An opinion should not say a condition "is service connected"; it should say whether it was caused or worsened by something, and why.
  • The standard is "at least as likely as not." That means a 50 percent or greater probability. When the evidence for and against is about evenly balanced, the law gives the benefit of the doubt to the veteran.
PhraseWhat it means to VA
"Is due to," "caused by," "more likely than not"Supports a link, above 50 percent.
"At least as likely as not"Supports a link, 50 percent or more. This meets the legal standard.
"Less likely than not"Against a link, below 50 percent.
"Cannot be resolved without resorting to mere speculation"Neither for nor against. Usable only if the examiner explains why, and what is missing.
"Could," "may," "possibly," with no reasonsUsually given little weight on its own. ClaimReady note, from general knowledge of court decisions.

Law 38 U.S.C. § 5107(b); 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.102, 3.303 VA guidance M21-1 V.ii.1.A.1; IV.i.2.A.7

The parts

What a well-supported opinion includes

VA's guidance says a rationale should cite the medical principles used, identify the evidence in the case it relied on, and show how the conclusion was reached. Courts treat an opinion like expert testimony: it should rest on enough facts, use reliable methods, and apply them to this person.

  1. Who is giving it.Credentials, specialty, and relationship to the patient. Treating doctors get no automatic extra weight, but knowing the history can add weight.
  2. What was reviewed.Records, tests, and literature. A private doctor does not have to review the VA claims file if the opinion rests on accurate, enough facts.
  3. The history, including the veteran's own account.What happened in service, symptoms since, and later events. An opinion based on the veteran's history is credible unless that history is shown to be wrong.
  4. The opinion, in VA's terms.One clear sentence using "at least as likely as not" or "less likely than not," for each theory asked: caused by, and, if raised, made worse by.
  5. The reasons.The medical principles and how they apply to these facts. This is where most of the weight comes from.
  6. The other side.Why contrary evidence, other causes, or an earlier opinion does or does not change the conclusion.

VA guidance M21-1 V.ii.1.A.3 Court Nieves-Rodriguez v. Peake, 22 Vet. App. 295 (2008); Stefl v. Nicholson, 21 Vet. App. 120 (2007); Kowalski v. Nicholson, 19 Vet. App. 171 (2005)

The veteran's own account

Lay statements and missing records

  • A veteran or witness is competent to describe what they saw, heard, or felt, including their own symptoms. VA must consider these statements.
  • A statement is not unbelievable just because no medical record backs it up.
  • An opinion that rests only on the absence of a record in service, and ignores the veteran's account of an injury, is inadequate. Absence of a record counts against a claim only if the fact would ordinarily have been recorded.

VA guidance M21-1 V.ii.1.A.2; V.ii.1.B.2 Court Buchanan v. Nicholson, 451 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2006); Dalton v. Nicholson, 21 Vet. App. 23 (2007)

"Without speculation"

When an examiner says it can't be answered

  • The phrase "cannot be resolved without resorting to mere speculation" is not a conclusion against the claim. It is no evidence either way.
  • VA can rely on it only if the examiner explains why, or the reason is clear from the record: for example, that medicine cannot answer it, or that specific facts are missing.
  • The examiner should show that all procurable, relevant information was considered. If a missing record or test is the problem, VA may be able to get it.

VA guidance M21-1 V.ii.1.A.5; IV.i.3.A Court Jones v. Shinseki, 23 Vet. App. 382 (2010)

What you can do

Tips for each reader

Veterans and families

  • Download your exam report on VA.gov and read the opinion and rationale.
  • Compare it to the problems table. Note page numbers.
  • Write a statement with the facts the examiner missed, in your own words.
  • If you ask your own doctor for an opinion, share this page and the reason VA denied the claim.

Doctors and clinicians

  • Say what you reviewed and how you know the patient.
  • Give the opinion in VA's terms, for each theory raised.
  • Explain the medical reasons. Cite literature where it helps.
  • Address the earlier VA opinion's reasoning directly.
  • Describe function and limits; leave legal labels to VA.
  • Many DBQ forms are public. What VA asks your examiner

Lawyers, agents, and VSOs

  • An inadequate VA exam is a duty to assist issue a Higher-Level Review can send back.
  • A new private opinion goes with a Supplemental Claim.
  • Examiners are presumed competent; a challenge must be raised. You can ask for the examiner's qualifications.
  • In a complex or disputed case, a written request for an independent medical opinion is possible.

Law 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.328, 3.2601(g) Court Francway v. Wilkie, 940 F.3d 1304 (Fed. Cir. 2019)

Example

Reading Dana's two exam opinions

Fictional example

Knee opinion: "Service treatment records … show a single sick-call visit in 2007 for a left knee strain, which resolved with rest. There is no documentation of a chronic knee condition in service … The veteran did not seek treatment until 2019."

  • It rests on the absence of records, and does not mention Dana's account of knee pain that continued after the 2007 injury.
  • It does not explain medically why a 2007 strain could not lead to the current condition.

Hearing opinion: "Hearing was within normal limits at separation … Because hearing was normal at discharge, there is no nexus. It is possible that occupational noise after service contributed."

  • Normal hearing at separation is the only reason given.
  • "It is possible" is a speculative alternative with no reasons.

These opinions were written for teaching. They are not real VA exam reports, and spotting a gap does not predict any outcome.

Common misunderstandings

What people often get wrong

"Two favorable opinions beat one negative one."
No. VA weighs opinions by their reasoning, not their number.
"My treating doctor's opinion wins automatically."
No. It gets no automatic extra weight, though knowing your history can help.
"A private doctor must review my whole VA file."
No. What matters is whether the opinion rests on accurate, enough facts.
"'Without speculation' means VA said no."
No. It counts neither way, and VA can rely on it only if the examiner explains it.
"A nexus letter only needs one sentence."
No. A conclusion without reasons carries little weight.
